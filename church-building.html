The Church Building

Discover the layers of history in St Michael and All Angels: early stonework, medieval craftsmanship and later changes.

Early fabric and later change

Historic England identifies 10th-century origins, with substantial late-12th-century work. The precise foundation date is uncertain: the parish account’s suggestion of around 950 is an interpretation of early fabric, not a documented building date.

The Norman chancel arch, raised in 1872, and the stone font connect the present church with its medieval past. The chancel has narrow lancet windows, but the three east lancets are 19th-century work. The west porch is dated to the 15th century; two medieval grave covers survive inside it.

Restoration in 1823 and extensive rebuilding in 1872 changed the church considerably. Our History timeline sets these changes beside the earlier fabric rather than treating the church as an unchanged medieval building.

The Norbury Chapel

The side chapel dates from around 1300 and has distinctive chequered flint and stone walls. Its chest tomb and brass figures commemorate William Wyddowsoun and his wife. Sources disagree on the memorial’s exact year, so it is described here as early 16th century.

The parish’s historical account links the chapel with the Stydolf family and later with the Locks of Norbury, who used it as a family pew. Historic England records 17th-century embossed wooden panels in the chapel.

Carving, furnishings and remembrance

The elaborately carved wooden pulpit was brought from Belgium in 1840, according to Historic England. The listing also records wooden panels beside the chancel reredos, probably 17th century and brought from Bohemia. Their age is an architectural assessment, not a known carving date.

The parish’s historical account dates the present reredos to 1938. It also records the four larger bells given by Dame Elizabeth Lawrence in 1913 in memory of her husband, Sir James Lawrence, completing the peal of six. These details are retained as the account’s record; the bells story includes a link to the historical film.

A tradition in the chancel

The parish account notes that the chancel is out of alignment with the nave. It traditionally interprets this as a “Weeping Chancel”, evoking Christ’s inclined head on the Cross. This is a symbolic reading of the building; it does not establish why the chancel was built at that angle.
